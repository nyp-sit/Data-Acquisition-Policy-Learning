Ten policies, one arm

MakerMods Lab can train nine LeRobot policies and run one more on the SO-101. They fall into three families that differ in one thing above all: who or what teaches the robot. This guide shows how each policy trains, what data it needs from your SO-101, and what it is good and bad at.

What every policy does

A policy is the function that turns what the robot senses into what it does next, 30 times a second. The inputs and outputs are the same for all ten; the difference is how each one learned to fill in the box in the middle.

Which fields of your SO-101 dataset each policy uses

Every LeRobot recording stores observation.images.front, observation.images.wrist, observation.state (6 joints) and action (6 targets) at 30 fps, plus a task string. A reward and live arm time are extras that only RL needs.

The robot learns from a reward: it tries, gets scored, and keeps what scored well. The teacher is a reward signal.

TD-MPC

Temporal Difference learning for Model Predictive Control · Hansen, Wang & Su (2022)

1 action per stepplans 5 steps aheadVRAM ≈ 2–6 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · no extra install

TD-MPC learns a small model of the world inside its own head: how the scene changes after an action, how much reward that brings, and how good the situation is in the long run. At every control step it imagines hundreds of short action plans with that model, keeps the best one, and executes only its first action.

How it trains

  1. Record transitions: observation, action, reward and next observation. The reward column is required.
  2. Encode each observation into a compact latent state z.
  3. Train three heads together: predict the next z, predict the reward, and estimate the long-term value Q (temporal-difference learning).
  4. At run time, the planner (MPPI) samples 512 action sequences × 6 refinement rounds over a 5-step horizon, inside the model, and sends the first action.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • Exactly one camera, cropped square (front camera is the usual choice).
  • Joint positions and actions, as in every dataset.
  • A reward for every frame, e.g. 1 when the cube is in the bin, 0 otherwise. Teleoperated SO-101 datasets do not record this, so it must be labelled.
  • Originally designed to keep learning online from the arm’s own tries; MakerMods Lab trains it offline on a recorded dataset only.

Advantages

  • Plans ahead instead of reacting, so it can recover from small errors.
  • Sample-efficient for an RL method: the world model squeezes more out of each transition.
  • Can in principle do better than the demonstrations, because it optimises reward rather than copying.

Disadvantages

  • Needs a reward on every frame, which SO-101 teleop data does not have.
  • One camera only, square images only: the wrist view is lost.
  • Heavy planning at every step (thousands of imagined rollouts) and many settings to tune.

Use it for: Tasks with an easy automatic score, mostly in simulation. On the real SO-101, an advanced option.

RL

Gaussian Actor

the policy half of Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) · Haarnoja et al. (2018); used in HIL-SERL, Luo et al. (2024)

1 action per steplearns from its own triesVRAM: config-dependentMakerMods Lab: Trainable (offline job) · no extra install

The Gaussian Actor is the part of Soft Actor-Critic that drives the arm. For each observation it outputs a mean and a spread for every joint, and samples an action from that bell curve. Separate critic networks learn to score actions from the rewards the robot actually received, and the actor is pushed towards actions the critics score highly while keeping some randomness so it keeps exploring.

How it trains

  1. Let the policy act on the arm (or in simulation) and store every transition (s, a, r, s′) in a replay buffer.
  2. Get a reward for each step: a success detector (reward classifier) or a person pressing a key.
  3. A human can take over with the leader arm when the robot heads somewhere unsafe; those corrections go into the buffer too (HIL-SERL).
  4. Train the critics to predict reward plus future value; train the actor to pick actions the critics like, with an entropy bonus for exploration.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • Joint positions plus one or more cropped camera views (small images keep training fast).
  • A reward on every step, from a reward classifier or a key press.
  • Live time on the arm: the method learns from its own attempts, usually an hour or more of supervised interaction.
  • Demonstrations can seed the buffer, but they are not enough on their own. MakerMods Lab offers only the offline training job and has removed the reward classifier.

Advantages

  • Can become more precise and faster than the human, because it optimises reward.
  • Learns from its own successes and failures, including recovery behaviour.
  • Small network, one fast forward pass per step at run time.

Disadvantages

  • Needs a reward on every step and live interaction with the arm.
  • A person must supervise every minute for safety: exploration on real hardware is risky.
  • Sensitive to reward design and settings; in MakerMods Lab it cannot run its online loop.

Use it for: Polishing a skill beyond what demos can teach (precise insertion), with a person supervising. Usually after IL, not instead of it.

ACT

Action Chunking with Transformers · Zhao et al. (2023)

chunk of 100 actions≈ 52 M parametersVRAM ≈ 2–6 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · no extra install

ACT is behaviour cloning built for low-cost arms like the SO-101. It looks at both cameras and the joints, and predicts a whole chunk of the next 100 joint targets at once, which makes motion smooth and cuts compounding error. During training a small extra encoder learns a ‘style’ variable so ACT can cope with demonstrations that differ slightly.

How it trains

  1. Pick a random frame from an episode: two images, six joint positions.
  2. Take the next 100 recorded actions from the same episode as the target chunk.
  3. ResNet-18 encoders and a transformer predict a chunk; the L1 loss (plus a small KL term for the style variable) measures how far it is from your demo.
  4. Repeat for every frame, many times. It trains from scratch apart from the ImageNet-pretrained image encoder.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • About 50 curated episodes of one task: 10 at each of 5 start positions.
  • Both cameras (front + wrist), joint positions and actions at 30 fps.
  • One consistent strategy: the same approach and grasp in every episode.
  • No task text, no reward. One model per task.

Advantages

  • Works from about 50 demonstrations and trains in under an hour or two on one GPU.
  • Smooth, decisive motion thanks to action chunks (and optional temporal ensembling).
  • Small and fast at run time: runs comfortably on a lab PC.

Disadvantages

  • One task per model; it cannot take an instruction.
  • Only knows places the demos went: covariate shift at unseen positions.
  • Limited multi-modality: two different strategies in the data can average into a bad motion.

Use it for: The default first policy on the SO-101: one pick-and-place task, learned from your own demonstrations.

IL

Diffusion Policy

Visuomotor policy learning via action diffusion · Chi et al. (2023)

predicts 64, runs 32100 denoising stepsVRAM ≈ 8–14 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · installs lerobot[diffusion]

Diffusion Policy borrows the idea behind image generators. It starts from pure noise the size of an action sequence and removes the noise step by step, guided by what the cameras see, until a clean trajectory appears. Because it generates rather than averages, it can keep several good ways of doing a task and commit to one of them.

How it trains

  1. Take a recorded action sequence (64 steps in this LeRobot fork) and the two most recent observations.
  2. Add a random amount of noise to the action sequence.
  3. A 1-D U-Net, conditioned on the image and joint features, predicts the noise that was added.
  4. The loss is the squared error between predicted and true noise. At run time the U-Net removes noise over 100 steps and the arm executes the first 32 actions.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • Demonstrations of one task, recorded as for ACT, with both cameras, joints and actions.
  • Trained from scratch, so plan for at least the ACT amount (≈ 50 episodes); more demos help.
  • Multiple strategies in the data are acceptable, unlike ACT, but stay consistent where you can.
  • Images are randomly cropped during training, so keep the cube well inside both views.

Advantages

  • Handles multi-modal demonstrations: commits to one good path instead of averaging into a crash.
  • Very smooth, stable trajectories; strong results on contact-rich tasks in the original paper.
  • Stable, well-understood training objective.

Disadvantages

  • Slow at run time: many denoising steps for every decision.
  • More compute to train (hours on one GPU) than ACT.
  • Trained from scratch, one task per model, no language.

Use it for: One task where people naturally demonstrate it in more than one way, and you can afford slower inference.

IL

VQ-BeT

Vector-Quantized Behavior Transformer · Lee et al. (2024)

chunks of 5 actions2-phase trainingVRAM ≈ 2–6 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · no extra install

VQ-BeT turns motion into a vocabulary. First it learns to compress short action chunks into a pair of discrete codes, like words. Then a GPT-style transformer learns to pick the right ‘word’ for what it sees, plus a small offset to fine-tune it. Picking a word, rather than averaging numbers, lets it keep several distinct behaviours.

How it trains

  1. Phase 1 (20,000 steps by default): a residual VQ-VAE learns to encode every 5-step action chunk as a coarse code plus a fine code (16 options each) and decode it back.
  2. Phase 2: freeze that vocabulary. A GPT transformer reads the last 5 observations.
  3. It predicts which code fits (a classification loss) and a continuous offset (a regression loss); the targets come from encoding your recorded chunk.
  4. At run time: predict code + offset, decode with the phase-1 decoder, execute the 5 actions.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • Exactly one camera: choose front or wrist (the code refuses more than one).
  • Joint positions and actions, with 5 frames of history per sample.
  • Demonstrations of one task, roughly the ACT amount; distinct strategies are handled better than by ACT.
  • No task text, no reward.

Advantages

  • Keeps several distinct behaviours: choosing a code is a decision, not an average.
  • Fast at run time: one forward pass, no denoising loop.
  • Small: trains on a laptop-class GPU.

Disadvantages

  • Two training phases and more settings to get right.
  • One camera only: on the SO-101 you give up the wrist view or the front view.
  • Short chunks (5 steps) mean more decisions per episode than ACT, so more chances to compound error.

Use it for: A small, fast multi-modal policy when one camera view is enough.

SmolVLA

a small vision-language-action model from Hugging Face · Shukor et al. (2025)

≈ 450 M parameterschunk of 50 actionsVRAM ≈ 10–16 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · installs lerobot[smolvla]

SmolVLA joins a small vision-language model (SmolVLM2) to an action expert. The backbone already knows what objects, colours and words look like; the action expert turns that understanding into a 50-step chunk of joint targets using flow matching. It was pre-trained on 481 community LeRobot datasets, mostly SO-100-class arms, so it starts out knowing arms like yours.

How it trains

  1. Start from the pre-trained smolvla_base, not from scratch.
  2. Each sample: two images (64 tokens each), the instruction text, the joint state, and the next 50 recorded actions.
  3. The frozen backbone turns images and words into features; the action expert learns to turn noise into your chunk (flow matching: predict the direction from noise to data).
  4. About 20,000 steps; only the action expert and small projectors (≈ 100 M of 450 M) are updated by default.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • About 50 episodes per task variant, spread over your start positions.
  • Both cameras, joints, actions, plus one exact instruction string per task.
  • The instruction must decide the action: both cubes on the table, “red” in half the episodes, “blue” in the other half.
  • Camera names mapped to the base model’s slots (rename_map); same lighting as the test scene.

Advantages

  • Several tasks in one model, chosen by a sentence.
  • Pre-training on SO-100-class data: fewer demos than training a VLA from scratch would need.
  • Small enough to fine-tune on one 24 GB GPU; designed for asynchronous inference.

Disadvantages

  • Slower than ACT on the arm; needs asynchronous inference to avoid stutter.
  • Can ignore the instruction if the data never forced it to listen.
  • Larger GPU and longer training than ACT (hours rather than minutes).

Use it for: Two or more related tasks on the SO-101 chosen by instruction, on a single lab or cloud GPU.

π0 (PI0)

a vision-language-action flow model from Physical Intelligence · Black et al. (2024)

≈ 3.3 B parameterschunk of 50 actionsVRAM ≈ 24–40 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · installs lerobot[pi]

π0 uses a much larger vision-language backbone (PaliGemma, about 3 B parameters) with a 300 M action expert that generates 50-step chunks by flow matching. It was pre-trained on over 10,000 hours of robot data from 7 robot configurations and 68 tasks, which gives it strong general manipulation skills before you fine-tune it on the SO-101.

How it trains

  1. Start from the pre-trained pi0_base.
  2. Same sample as SmolVLA: images, instruction, joint state, next 50 recorded actions.
  3. Flow matching: the action expert learns the direction from noise to your chunk; at run time it takes 10 steps.
  4. Fine-tuning updates much more of the model than SmolVLA’s default, so it needs a large GPU; LoRA (small add-on matrices) reduces memory.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • About 50 episodes per task variant, both cameras, joints, actions and exact instruction text, as for SmolVLA.
  • The same paired-scene rule: the sentence must decide which cube moves.
  • More data and variety let the big model show its advantage; with very few episodes it can overfit.
  • Plan a cloud GPU: an A100 40 GB for about 4–8 hours for a 50-episode dataset.

Advantages

  • Strong pre-trained manipulation skills from a large multi-robot dataset.
  • Smooth, dexterous motion; good at contact-rich tasks.
  • Large language backbone: follows varied instructions better than small models.

Disadvantages

  • Needs a 40 GB-class GPU to fine-tune comfortably; will not train on lab PCs.
  • Slower inference on modest hardware: plan for asynchronous inference.
  • Harder to iterate quickly: each experiment costs hours of cloud time.

Use it for: When SmolVLA is not enough and you have cloud GPU budget: harder or more varied tasks.

VLA

π0-FAST (PI0 Fast)

π0 with the FAST action tokenizer · Pertsch et al. (2025)

actions as tokens≈ 3 B parametersVRAM ≈ 24–40 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · installs lerobot[pi]

π0-FAST keeps π0’s vision-language backbone but treats actions like text. The FAST tokenizer compresses each action chunk into a short sequence of tokens (a frequency transform, then byte-pair encoding), and the language model learns to write those tokens one at a time, exactly like predicting the next word.

How it trains

  1. Compress your recorded 50-step chunk: DCT (a frequency transform like JPEG), quantise, then byte-pair encoding, giving up to 256 tokens.
  2. The vision-language model reads images, instruction and state, and predicts the action tokens one by one.
  3. The loss is ordinary next-token cross-entropy, the same objective the language model was built for.
  4. At run time it writes the tokens one at a time, then the FAST decoder turns them back into a chunk.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • As for π0: about 50 episodes per variant, both cameras, joints, actions and exact instruction text.
  • Paired scenes so the instruction decides the action.
  • Consistent, smooth demos compress into fewer tokens and are easier to learn.
  • Same large-GPU requirement as π0 for fine-tuning.

Advantages

  • Trains efficiently: the authors report up to 5× less training time than diffusion-style π0.
  • Uses the language model’s native skill, next-token prediction, which helps instruction following.
  • Compact action representation for high-frequency, smooth motion.

Disadvantages

  • Slow at run time: tokens are generated one after another (autoregressive).
  • A garbled token sequence decodes into a poor chunk.
  • Same heavy GPU needs as π0.

Use it for: Training-time efficiency for a big VLA when slower run-time decisions are acceptable.

VLA

π0.5 (PI0.5)

a VLA with open-world generalisation, from Physical Intelligence · Physical Intelligence (2025)

≈ 3 B parameterschunk of 50 actionsVRAM ≈ 24–40 GBMakerMods Lab: Trainable · installs lerobot[pi]

π0.5 is π0’s successor, trained to work in places it has never seen. Its backbone is co-trained on a mix of robot demonstrations from many robots, web images and text, and high-level subtask labels, so it can break ‘clean the kitchen’ into ‘pick up the plate’ and then produce the motion with a flow-matching action expert.

How it trains

  1. Start from pi05_base, which was co-trained on robot data, web data and subtask labels.
  2. Fine-tune on your episodes: images, instruction, state and the next 50 actions.
  3. The backbone learns from discretised action tokens while the action expert learns flow matching; a stop-gradient keeps the expert from disturbing what the backbone knows (‘knowledge insulation’).
  4. At run time it can first predict a subtask in words, then generate the chunk with the expert.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • About 50 episodes per variant with both cameras, joints, actions and exact instruction text.
  • Paired scenes so the instruction decides the action.
  • Variety helps it most: different positions, lighting and backgrounds.
  • Cloud GPU: LeRobot’s guide gives about 4–8 h on an A100 40 GB for 5 epochs of a 50-episode dataset.

Advantages

  • The strongest generalisation of the set: new scenes, objects and layouts.
  • Handles longer, multi-step tasks through subtask prediction.
  • Pre-training on web data keeps broad knowledge of objects and words.

Disadvantages

  • The heaviest to fine-tune: 40 GB-class GPU, hours per run.
  • Slow on modest hardware; needs asynchronous inference.
  • Large and complex: harder to debug when it fails.

Use it for: Varied scenes or multi-step tasks where generalisation matters more than compute cost.

VLA

MolmoAct2

an action reasoning model from Ai2 · Ai2 (2026)

chunk of 30 actionsreasons in 3-Drun-only in the LabMakerMods Lab: Run only (released checkpoints) · installs lerobot[molmoact2]

MolmoAct2 is built on Molmo 2-ER, a vision-language model given extra embodied-reasoning training. It reasons about the 3-D scene (the Think variant adds depth-perception tokens) and passes that understanding to a flow-matching action expert through a KV-cache bridge. Its training mix includes large-scale SO-100/SO-101 data.

How it trains

  1. Pre-trained by Ai2 on 700+ hours of bimanual demos plus SO-100/101, DROID and Open X-Embodiment subsets, with about 146K re-annotated task labels.
  2. The backbone reasons about the scene; the Think variant predicts depth tokens only where the scene changes (adaptive depth).
  3. A flow-matching action expert reads the backbone’s cached features and produces continuous actions.
  4. In MakerMods Lab you deploy released checkpoints; fine-tuning is done with Ai2’s scripts or the LeRobot command line.

Data it needs on the SO-101

  • To run a released checkpoint: your cameras, joint state and an instruction, matched to the checkpoint’s expected inputs.
  • To fine-tune (outside the Lab): demonstrations with exact instruction text, like the other VLAs.
  • Optional visual traces can guide the path the robot should take.
  • Out-of-the-box support is limited to SO-100/101, bimanual YAM and Franka set-ups.

Advantages

  • Explicit 3-D spatial reasoning; strong zero-shot results reported by Ai2.
  • Open weights, data and training code.
  • SO-100/101 data in pre-training, so it already knows arms like ours.

Disadvantages

  • Cannot be trained inside MakerMods Lab: run-only.
  • Plans 10–30 moves at a time without re-checking, so it reacts slowly to surprises; transitions can look jerky.
  • Large model; depth reasoning adds latency (about 180 ms per call, 790 ms with adaptive depth, on an H100).

Use it for: Trying a strong pre-trained model zero-shot on the SO-101, or as a comparison point for your own fine-tunes.

Which one should I try first?

1

One task, demonstrated with the leader arm? Start with ACT. If people demonstrate it in more than one way, try Diffusion Policy; if one camera is enough and you want a small fast model, VQ-BeT.

2

Several related tasks chosen by a sentence? Fine-tune SmolVLA on one GPU. Move up to π0.5 or π0 only with cloud GPU budget and more varied data.

3

Can success be scored automatically, and can someone supervise live training? Then RL (Gaussian Actor, TD-MPC) can polish a skill beyond the demos, usually after IL, and best in simulation first.

4

Want to see what a strong pre-trained model does without training? Run a released MolmoAct2 checkpoint as a comparison point.
